🧠 Phase 3 — Mnemonic creation session (30 min)
Build one mnemonic per hard-to-remember list · mix all three types based on content · write every one in your diary
This is dedicated time to turn the content from your summary sheets into memory devices. You are not reviewing — you are engineering shortcuts your brain can retrieve fast under exam pressure. Use all three types depending on what the content is:

Type 1 — Acronyms (best for ordered lists and classifications)
Take the first letter of each item and form a word or phrase. The stranger and more vivid the better — your brain holds weird things better than neutral ones.

Week 1 example — PL Art. 55 offense classifications (top to bottom):
A B C D E · A B V → "Always Bring Cold Drinks Every Afternoon Before Visiting"
(Class A Felony, B Felony, C Felony, D Felony, E Felony, A Misdemeanor, B Misdemeanor, Violation)
Write this phrase on your summary sheet next to the classification ladder. Add an Anki card: front = "What's the mnemonic for the offense classification order?" Back = the phrase + the full list.

Week 1 example — PL Art. 10 mental states (in order of culpability, most to least):
I K R N → "I Know Real Negligence"
(Intentionally, Knowingly, Recklessly, criminal Negligence)

Type 2 — Story / link method (best for sequences, procedures, and relationships)
Create a short vivid story where each element represents the next piece of information in order. The more absurd and visual the story, the better it sticks.

Week 1 example — CPL Art. 30 speedy trial time frames:
Picture a clock (30 days) running a race. It trips after 30 steps and grabs a calendar showing 90 to steady itself. It finishes the race at a finish line marked 180 — exactly six months after it started.
Every time you think "speedy trial," run that story. 30 → 90 → 180 in sequence, visualized.

Type 3 — Rhymes and phrases (best for numbers, deadlines, and single facts)
Short, rhythmic phrases are surprisingly durable in memory — your brain processes rhythm differently from plain text.

Week 1 examples:
"Thirty days, then ninety more — one eighty's when they close the door." (CPL Art. 30 speedy trial ladder)
"Intentional means you meant it, knowing means you knew — reckless is the gamble, negligence slipped through." (PL Art. 10 mental states in plain English)

How to build your mnemonic each Saturday:
1. Look at your new summary sheets. Circle every list, every ordered sequence, every number cluster.
2. For each circle: decide which type fits — acronym for lists, story for sequences, rhyme for numbers.
3. Write the mnemonic next to the circled content on your summary sheet.
4. Write it again in a dedicated Mnemonic Master List page in your diary (one running page you add to each week).
5. Add one Anki card per mnemonic: front = the question, back = the mnemonic + what it stands for.
6. Test it immediately: close the summary sheet and try to recite the list using only the mnemonic. If you can't, the mnemonic isn't vivid enough — revise it until it works.

Every method explained — what it is, why it works, exactly when to use it
✍️
Free recall / blurting
From Reddit (r/GetStudying)
When: Daily lunch (10 min) · Friday lunch (20 min) · Sunday Pomodoro #1
You write down everything you know about a topic on blank paper before looking at any notes. Then you check and fill gaps in a different color.

Why it works: Retrieval practice — the act of pulling information out of your brain — strengthens the neural pathway far more than re-reading does. Re-reading creates familiarity, not recall. On exam day, you need recall. Blurting trains exactly that. The Reddit poster (Weak-One2521) went from B/C average to A/A+ using this as the core technique. Never re-read notes immediately before blurting — it cues the information artificially and defeats the purpose.
🔁
Expanding gap spaced repetition
From Reddit (r/GetStudying) + Anki algorithm
When: Built into every lunch blurt · Anki automates card gaps · Diary tracks long-gap reviews
You review material at increasing time intervals: same day → 1 day → 3 days → 1 week → 3 weeks → 2 months → etc. The gap widens each time you successfully recall.

Why it works: The science (Ebbinghaus forgetting curve) shows that reviewing just before you would forget something produces far stronger retention than reviewing immediately after learning. Anki calculates this automatically for cards. Your diary handles the longer gaps (15 days, 31 days, 63 days) that Anki may not reach before the exam. The Reddit poster memorized 200+ poems using this exact calendar-diary method.
📓
Spaced repetition diary (year planner)
From Reddit (poem memorization post)
When: Write entry same day you learn each article · Check daily for scheduled reviews · Update Saturday
In a physical planner or diary, write the date you learned each article and then write the exact future dates you will review it: +1 day, +3 days, +15 days, +31 days, +63 days, +127 days. Those dates go in your calendar.

Why it works: Anki handles short-gap card reviews well, but it won't schedule a full article blurt at 63 days out. The diary does. It also gives you a visible record of how much you've covered, which reduces anxiety and helps you plan. The Reddit poster used this to memorize 200 poems verbatim and reach the top 100 competitive mnemonists globally. The system is simple — the discipline is in writing the entry every single time you learn something new.
🗣
Active recall micro-practice
From Reddit (poem memorization post)
When: Shower · Elevator · Walking to train · Waiting · Any idle moment
During any non-studying moment — shower, toilet, walking, eating, commute rest time — you mentally or verbally recite what you've learned. No phone, no notes. Just retrieval from memory.

Why it works: The Reddit poster's key insight is that the brain decides what to store long-term based on how often you signal that information is important. Passive exposure (re-reading, listening) does not send that signal strongly. Actively reciting — especially out loud — tells your brain this information matters and routes it from short-term to long-term memory. After a few months, this practice makes you measurably faster at memorization. It requires zero extra time because you do it during time you already have.
📖
Layered reading
From the Reddit study document (shared in conversation)
When: Every night wind-down (previewing next day's article) · Sunday morning Bluemedia book read
Instead of reading a chapter start to finish, you read in layers: index → end questions → summary → introduction → full text. Each pass gives you a progressively detailed picture.

Why it works: When you read the index and end questions first, your brain knows what to look for before it reaches the full text. This converts passive reading into active searching — you're hunting for answers, not consuming information. The document notes that text in bold and italic is typically exam-critical; layered reading helps you identify those sections faster. For law articles, reading the questions first tells you which definitions and procedures are tested, so when you hit them in the full text they register as important.
⏱
Pomodoro technique (25+5 min)
Established method · Used Sunday deep sessions
When: Sunday only — 3 blocks. Commute sessions use a simpler 20/30-min cap instead.
You work for exactly 25 minutes on one task, then take a mandatory 5-minute break before the next block. After 4 blocks, take a longer 20-minute break.

Why it works: Sustained attention degrades after roughly 25–30 minutes on a single cognitive task. The break resets your prefrontal cortex so the next block starts at full capacity. Working for 2 hours straight produces less output than three 25-minute blocks with breaks. It also prevents the specific fatigue you mentioned — screen tiredness — because each break is a genuine cognitive reset. Use a phone timer app. Do not use a browser tab — the temptation to check other tabs defeats the focus.
🏛
Roman Room / Memory Palace
From the Reddit study document + Reddit memory post
When: Thursday lunch (build) · Saturday notes session (extend + map) · Sunday Pomodoro #3 (walk)
You mentally place items you want to memorize on specific objects in a familiar location (your bedroom, kitchen, etc.) and visualize them interacting dramatically. You then recall them by mentally walking the route in order.

Why it works: Your spatial memory is one of the most powerful memory systems you have — it's why you can navigate places you've visited once but forget a list you read yesterday. Attaching abstract information (statute numbers, mental states, classifications) to spatial locations converts it into something your brain is already excellent at storing. The Reddit poster's important caveat: the palace itself requires spaced repetition. Walking it mentally on a schedule — just like your diary — is what prevents the images from fading. This is why Pomodoro #3 every Sunday includes a palace walk.
🚶
Move + sleep
From the Reddit study document (John Ratey — Spark)
When: Friday lunch walk (mandatory) · Screens off 1 hr before bed every night · Dark, ventilated room
Regular physical movement and quality sleep are not lifestyle tips — they are direct inputs to memory formation. The document cites research by John Ratey showing that students who exercise regularly score roughly 20% better than those who don't.

Why it works: Exercise increases BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor), which promotes the growth of new neural connections — the physical substrate of learning. Sleep is when your brain consolidates the day's learning: it replays and encodes memories during deep sleep. Cutting sleep short literally deletes what you studied that day. Screens before bed suppress melatonin and delay deep sleep onset. For a 20-week exam prep, protecting sleep is one of the highest-leverage things you can do — more than adding another 30 minutes of studying.
